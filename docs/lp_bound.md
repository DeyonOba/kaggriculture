# The LP bound: how much money is actually on the table?

`tools/lp_bound.py` — 2026-09-14.

Every lever we have measured this month (opening, crop mix, herd, land, hands, assignment, sells,
delivery, harvest timing) came back flat or negative, and the replay tapes beat our planner by
$20k+. Before spending three weeks building an opening/plan search engine, the question worth
answering first is the cheap one: **ignoring routing, weeds and execution, how much money can a
farm possibly make in 30 days?** If the answer is near the best tape's $126,474 then no planner can
beat the tape and the only lever is throughput. If it is far above, the headroom is real.

This is that calculation. It is an LP, not a simulation, and it is deliberately generous.

## What is modelled

An activity-based linear program over 30 days. One column per

* `(crop, plant day, harvest age, fertilizer regime)`
* `(animal kind, placement day, care regime)`
* hire bracket per day (the fib cost curve is convex, so the LP fills it cheapest-first)
* land parcel per day (**integer** — the relaxation would otherwise buy 5% of a quadrant)
* `(product, price band, day)` sale

Each crop/animal column carries a per-day labour, yield, and input profile generated by simulating
the engine's own rules. The generator reproduces the README's yield table exactly — wheat 0.80,
carrot 0.75, tomato 0.33, strawberry 0.24, melon 0.55 units per tile-day — which is the check that
the profiles are right.

Four constraints bind:

| constraint | statement |
| --- | --- |
| tiles | occupancy ≤ 25 × (1 + quadrants bought so far) |
| labour | actions ≤ 24 × `eff` × (1 + hands), where `eff` is productive actions per worker-turn |
| cash | bank ≥ 0 every day, carried day to day |
| market | revenue walks a concave price ladder, in order |

## The market model, and why it is trustworthy

Revenue is bounded product by product. For any feasible schedule, the k-th unit we sell all season,
sold on day *d*, faces a market inventory of at least

```
I0 − (drain realised by day d) + (opponent's sales so far) + (k − 1)
```

because town consumption is the only thing that lowers inventory and it has not all happened yet.
Price falls with inventory, so pricing that unit at `price(I0 − cum_drain[d] + k − 1)` can only
overstate what it fetches. Bands partition k; each band fills once, and an ordering constraint makes
the ladder walk downward in time so the LP cannot sell a cheap band early and a dear one late.

**Validation.** Replaying the LP's own sell schedule through the engine's exact inventory recursion
(`econ.sell_marginal_prices`, the mirror of `_commit_unit`) gives revenue within **3.3%** of what the
ladder credited. The market side is sound; the numbers below are not an artefact of the relaxation.

Note the ordering constraint turned out not to bind — the LP was already walking the ladder in
order — so it costs nothing and removes a whole class of doubt.

## What is relaxed

Integrality of tiles, animals and hands; movement (a worker-turn becomes an action at rate `eff`);
weeds; the 10-orders-per-turn cap; within-day timing. All of these push the number **up**, which is
what an upper bound wants.

## Results

### 1. The market absorption ceiling

Sell every band at the best day's price, with no labour, tile or cash limit at all:

| scenario | ceiling |
| --- | --- |
| opponent sells nothing | **$512,675** |
| opponent mirrors our supply | **$286,075** |

Selling into a shared ladder costs 44% of the theoretical market before a single labour constraint
is applied. That is the single largest term in this game's economics.

### 2. Bound by labour efficiency

`eff` is productive actions per worker-turn, measured from the action census (ours 2,818 / ~6,800
unit-turns; top meta 3,391 / ~6,800). `eff = 1.0` means every worker-turn is a productive action and
nobody ever walks — physically impossible, included as the ceiling of the whole dimension.

| eff | hands/day | solo | vs mirrored opponent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.000 | 6.1 | $292,246 | **$171,860** |
| 0.750 | 8.1 | $289,055 | $168,458 |
| 0.499 | 10.5 | $271,241 | **$154,642** ← top meta (measured) |
| 0.414 | 10.9 | $255,136 | **$147,020** ← ours (measured) |
| 0.350 | 10.8 | $238,036 | $141,466 |
| 0.300 | 10.7 | $219,736 | $135,624 |

**This is the most important table here.** Under competition, closing the entire measured throughput
gap to the top meta is worth **+$7.6k**, and taking routing all the way to physical perfection —
zero movement, every turn productive — is worth **+$24.8k**. The executor is the binding constraint
on 29 of 30 days, and it is *still* only worth that much, because the ladder the extra output has to
be sold into is shared.

### 3. The competitive bound (the number that matters)

Iterated best response against an opponent who supplies the same market on the same timetable.
Best-responding to the previous round alone oscillates wildly (flood the premium ladders, then flee
them), so the opponent plays the average of every plan we have played — fictitious play, which
converges:

| round | final bank | dominant products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| solo | $255,031 | strawberry $95k, milk $77k, wool $56k, melon $33k |
| mirror 1 | $99,126 | egg $24k, milk $17k, wheat $15k |
| mirror 2 | $158,225 | strawberry $48k, milk $36k, wool $27k |
| mirror 3 | $153,096 | strawberry $42k, milk $37k, wool $29k |
| … | … | … |
| mirror 9 | **$147,111** | strawberry $42k, milk $37k, wool $29k, melon $15k |

**So the bracket is: $147k against an opponent as strong as the LP itself, $255k against an opponent
who sells nothing.** Real 2500+ agents sit between those. The best tape records $126,474.

## Reading

**Both remaining levers are small, and they are the same size.**

| | value |
| --- | --- |
| a *perfect* planner at our throughput, over the best tape | $147,020 − $126,474 = **+$20.5k** |
| closing the measured throughput gap to the top meta | $154,642 − $147,020 = **+$7.6k** |
| taking routing to physical perfection (zero movement) | $171,860 − $147,020 = **+$24.8k** |

Neither dimension contains the $30-40k gap on its own, and both are upper bounds under relaxations
that all favour us, so the achievable share of each is smaller again. A planner that is infinitely
better than anything we could build is worth about as much as a router that never takes a step.

**The opponent term dominates everything.** Solo $255k versus mirrored $147k is a bigger swing than
every lever we have tested this month combined. This is the same conclusion the proxy-metric work
reached from the other direction ("money comes from selling into a shared market against an
opponent"), now with a number on it. It also explains the flat landscapes: any change that raises
our output raises the supply into a ladder the opponent is already flooding, and the ladder gives
most of it back.

**Labour binds on 29 of 30 days; tiles bind on 16.** Shadow price of one extra action is $13 on
average and $27 at the peak (day 25). So the executor *is* the binding constraint almost everywhere
— and per the table above, relieving it completely is still only worth $25k. Both things are true.

### Verdict on the opening/plan search engine

**Don't build it.** The kill criterion set before running this was: if the capacity-constrained
optimum lands near the tape's $126k, the program is dead; if comfortably above, the headroom is
real. $147k is above but not comfortably — +17%, as a generous upper bound, for three weeks of work
with 16 days left on the clock and a selector that is currently paying +$25-28k a game.

## The converged plan, versus the mined champion medians

The LP reaches this from first principles with no reference to any replay. The right-hand column is
`docs/knob_mining_2026-09-12.txt`, medians over 1,857 seats rated ≥2900:

| | LP | champions (0/1/2 consumers) | ours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| wheat plantings | **154** | 139 / 148 / 156 | 104 |
| carrots | 44 | 22 / 51 / 73 | — |
| strawberries | 29 | 21 / 29 / 35 | 33 |
| tomatoes | 17 | 4 / 9 / 14 | 0 |
| cows | 5.9 | 6 / 9 / 12 | 8 |
| sheep | 4.8 | 5 / 12 / 18 | 8-10 |
| geese | 5.0 | 2 / 3 / 5 | 0 |

That it lands on the champions' build order without being shown one is the strongest evidence that
the model is economically faithful. It also says our wheat volume (104 against their 148 and the
LP's 154) is genuinely low, and that the earlier reading of their extra plantings as "a COST" was
wrong — the LP chooses a mixed rotation, 105 tiles at full age-4 and 49 at ages 2-3.

**Why wheat is the crop the LP wants most, and why the demand book failed.** The 154 plantings yield
about 703 units, of which only 290 are sold. The other ~440 are eaten: 15.7 animals × ~28 days of
feed. Wheat is not a sales crop in this plan, it is the feed infrastructure that lets the herd
produce milk, wool and fertilizer at all. The demand book raised carrots 37→126 and tomatoes 0→42
but cut **wheat 181→100**, and scored +$231 ± 3,186 — it reallocated away from the one crop that
underwrites everything else. That is a specific, testable explanation for a result we previously
filed as "the knob space does not contain the answer."

Fertilizer on ongoing crops and daily CARE on every animal are taken at 100%, because both roughly

Two things to check against the shipped agent rather than assume:

* the wheat fertilize gate is `fert_price < 40`, but the LP's own plan drives fertilizer to ~$57

* the LP takes 5 geese with full care; our measured result is that geese cost us $30k because the
  daily load breaks cow and sheep care. That is an *executor* failure, not an economic one — the LP
  says the birds are sound if you can service them

Neither is a finding yet. Both are hypotheses the LP generated, and the gate for them is unchanged:
paired margin against `pub-*` on a fresh seed block.

## Gate: wheat volume — REJECTED (2026-09-14)

The wheat hypothesis was gated first, because the trace behind it was the strongest. Variants built
with `tools/make_knob_variant.py` from the frozen `champ-h8` bundle (one literal changed, one diff
hunk), paired against three external agents on identical seed/swap keys, 24 worlds each:

| variant | vs ahmed | vs tschinkel | vs boatlee | pooled |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `wheat_engine` 40 | −$1,111 | +$1,460 | +$1,557 | +$635 ± 735 (45/72) |
| `wheat_engine` 48 | −$1,667 | +$168 | +$2,253 | +$251 ± 755 (36/72) |
| `wheat_engine` 56 | −$1,736 | +$292 | +$2,243 | +$266 ± 757 (38/72) |

All three are **negative against `pub-ahmed-v38`** — the strongest opponent and the one we lose most
to — and the pooled mean is inside one standard error of zero. **Rejected.**

### Why the trace looked so convincing and the gate still says no

The trace that motivated this (3 games, champ-h8 vs ahmed) is real and still stands:

| | champ-h8 | pub-ahmed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| wheat planted | 104 | 163 |
| wheat sold | 174 @ $45 | 392 @ $44 |
| wheat bought | **225** @ $40 | 163 @ $35 |
| feed actions | 353 | 338 |
| net wheat position | **−$1,237** | **+$11,708** |

We feed the same size herd and are a net *importer* of wheat. But raising the knob does not fix it,
for two reasons the trace of the variants makes plain:

1. **The knob saturates.** `wheat_target = min(knobs["wheat_engine"], crops + free)` is capped by
   free tiles, so 48 and 56 produce the same farm: 118.3 and 119.0 plantings. The effective range
   tested was 104 → 119. **The LP's 154 was never actually reached**, and cannot be by this knob —
   it needs tiles, which means the third quadrant or fewer strawberries, i.e. a joint change, and
   the joint plan search over exactly these knobs already came back flat to noise.
2. **The extra wheat went to sales, not to feed.** Plantings 104 → 119 moved wheat *sold* 174 → 226
   while wheat *bought* stayed put at 225 → 237. The LP's mechanism — grow feed instead of importing
   it — never engaged. `plan.wheat_reserve = max(animals + 2, feed_days × animals)` is ~28 units at
   `feed_days` 2, so everything above 28 is sold and we buy back between harvests regardless of how
   much we grow.
3. Carrot plantings fell 24 → 14. The wheat tiles came straight out of carrots — the same
   reallocation-of-fixed-capacity failure the demand book hit.

So the LP's *diagnosis* survives (wheat is feed infrastructure, we under-produce it, and it costs us
~$13k a game against ahmed) while its *prescription* is not reachable through this knob. The open
follow-up is `feed_days`, which is what actually decides whether grown wheat is banked as feed or
dumped — untested above 2, and a different hypothesis from this one.

## Caveats

* `eff` is measured from *our* routing on *our* plan; a different plan has different movement. The
  sweep brackets this rather than resolving it.
* The competitive number is an upper bound *conditional on* the opponent supplying that schedule. It
  is not a bound against an arbitrary opponent.
* Expected shop draw by default. `--shops YARN_STORE,...` pins a specific draw.
* Bands are priced at their first unit, which over-credits slightly; the 3.3% replay gap is the
  total size of that effect.
* The hire ladder defaults to 12 brackets. Raising it to 18 lets the LP take 14.5 hands on peak days
  and moves the solo bound from $255,031 to $258,137 (+1.2%), so the cap is not doing real work —
  the fib cost curve self-limits well before it.

## Usage

```bash
.venv/bin/python tools/lp_bound.py --eff 0.414            # solo bound, full report
.venv/bin/python tools/lp_bound.py --eff 0.414 --mirror 9 # competitive, fictitious play
.venv/bin/python tools/lp_bound.py --sweep --mirror 8     # efficiency sensitivity
.venv/bin/python tools/lp_bound.py --shops YARN_STORE,PET_CAFE --eff 0.5
```

Roughly 15 s per solve (~20k columns, HiGHS), a couple of minutes for a converged mirror.
